On Oct 22, 2026, the Sol Finder scanner named a pump.fun coin called php as a call. Fourteen minutes later it was down about 80% after costs. This case study walks through what happened and the three lessons it illustrates. It is a paper call, written up for education.

Lesson 1: the first minutes mislead (the 5-minute hold)

php hit its high at about 3 minutes old while bots were trading it, then gave almost all of it back. A call made in that window is a call on the bots, not on the coin. Minute-3 and minute-5 holder counts for php weren't recorded, so it can't be proven that the survival check would have failed it, but this is exactly the pattern the 5-minute hold and its survival test (price at least 60% of the high, buyers ahead of sellers, holders rising from minute 3 to 5) are designed to catch.

Lesson 2: watch the creator, not the chart (the dev-sell kill)

When a coin collapses this fast, the first question is whether the creator, or wallets the creator funded, were selling. Answering it after the fact is too late; the dev-sell kill means it is checked during the hold, and any such sell ends the call.

Lesson 3: followers are not engagement

A rule like "a post from a 10k+ follower account" would have passed php: its creator had about 12.4k followers. Follower count says nothing about whether anyone cared about this coin, and a creator's own post shouldn't count at all. A better test is a post that isn't from the dev or project, includes the exact contract, and has 50+ likes or 5k+ views, and even then it is only logged, not used as a gate.

One more detail

php was named at about $13.2k, just under the roughly $15k–$40k band the desk rulebook uses. It is the first loss on the paper scoreboard.

A post-mortem of a paper call, not a recommendation about php or any other coin. Most new tokens lose most of their value.
