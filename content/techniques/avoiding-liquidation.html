How it works

On a leveraged perpetual or futures venue, liquidation is what happens when account equity can no longer support maintenance margin. The venue’s engine closes or transfers the position, often at a worse price than a stop you had in mind, and fees or a liquidation penalty may apply. Official rulebooks differ. Hyperliquid’s liquidation docs describe how that venue uses mark price, maintenance margin and a liquidation process on HyperCore (the perp book beside HyperEVM). Centralized exchanges publish their own formulas. Read the venue you would actually use; do not assume one rulebook applies to another.

Methods that try to stay away from that engine usually combine:

  1. Lower leverage than the maximum the venue allows, so ordinary noise does not sit next to the liquidation price. Changing leverage on an open position can move that price; some playbooks treat a leverage change as a new risk decision, not a cosmetic switch.
  2. A venue-resting stop well inside the liquidation price, not beyond it. See stop-loss and take-profit ladders.
  3. Size from stop distance and equity, so the planned loss is a small slice of the account, not the whole margin pile. See position sizing.
  4. A daily loss brake and no “revenge” add-ons after a stop. See the daily loss brake.
  5. Operational guards that published desk playbooks often list: margin-ratio alerts, a reduce-only flatten if margin goes through a pre-set line, and a dead-man’s switch if the trading process disconnects (so a stale position is not left unmanaged).
  6. Isolated vs cross margin awareness. Cross margin can keep one position alive with equity from another, and can also drag the whole account into one liquidation.

For hedged books (funding carry or basis), liquidation of one leg is still a failure — the surviving leg is then directional. See funding-rate carry.

When it fails

  • Gaps and thin marks. The mark price can jump through both your stop and the liquidation threshold.
  • The stop is only in a bot. If the process dies, the venue will still liquidate you.
  • Adding to a loser or raising leverage to “make room,” which often moves the liquidation price closer.
  • Funding, fees and borrow slowly eat margin even when the chart looks flat.
  • Auto-deleveraging or insurance-fund events on some venues, which can close or haircut winners as well as losers. Check the venue’s own docs.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not any venue’s live formula). A trader has $2,000 equity and opens a long with 10× gross leverage, so the notional is $20,000. If maintenance margin were 2.5% of notional (a round classroom figure, not a quoted venue rate), the position would be in trouble once equity fell near $500, which is a 7.5% adverse move on the asset from entry, before fees and funding. A planned stop 3% below entry, if it actually fills, would lose 3% × $20,000 = $600 (30% of equity) — already a large hit, and still better than handing the tail to the liquidation engine. If the trader had sized a $50 risk (0.25% of a larger account, or 2.5% of this $2,000) against a 3% stop, the notional would have been only about $1,667, which is well under 10× on this equity. The leverage cap and the sizing rule are doing different jobs.

Risks

  • Nothing in this page can make leverage safe. Liquidation can still happen faster than you can act.
  • Classroom margin percentages are not a substitute for the venue’s current formula.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to use leverage or to trade perps.
