How it works

Position sizing is the step that turns a risk limit into an order size. A common method is to decide, before entry, the largest loss you will accept on that trade as a percentage of account equity, then work backwards from the distance to your stop.

In outline:

  1. Pick a maximum dollar risk for the trade (for example a small percentage of current equity). This is a planning number, not a promise that the loss will stop there.
  2. Choose a stop level using a rule you can write down, such as a structure level or a volatility band. See stop-loss and take-profit ladders.
  3. Position size ≈ dollar risk ÷ distance from entry to stop (as a fraction of entry). A wider stop means a smaller position for the same dollar risk.
  4. Cap size against liquidity (can you exit without moving the market a large amount?) and against any venue leverage or open-risk ceiling you use.
  5. Keep a daily loss limit so a cluster of stops cannot drain the account in one session. That idea is expanded in the daily loss brake and the desk rulebook pages.

Published trading-desk playbooks often start example per-trade risk in a low range such as 0.25–0.5% of equity, with hard ceilings above that (for example 2% per trade, a cap on total open risk, a maximum leverage, and a daily loss brake). Those figures are published method examples, not advice to copy. Many desks also require the stop to rest on the exchange, not only in a spreadsheet, because a local bot can disconnect.

The same arithmetic applies on a DEX or a centralized exchange. On-chain, the “entry” you use in the formula should include expected fees and slippage; a tight stop that looks 2% away can already be inside a 3%+ round-trip cost. That problem shows up often on Solana memecoin routes — see the small-size trading-costs guide.

When it fails

  • The stop is skipped. Gaps, thin books and liquidation cascades can fill you far beyond the planned stop. Dollar risk was a plan, not a guaranteed maximum.
  • The size ignores liquidity. A size that is fine on a major CEX pair can be un-exitable in a new token pool.
  • Fees eat the edge. If the target is smaller than round-trip fees, even a “correct” size loses.
  • Risk is stacked. Five “0.5%” trades that are the same bet counted five times are not 0.5% risk.
  • Leverage hides the stop distance. Raising leverage without shrinking size increases dollar risk, even if the stop price is unchanged. See avoiding liquidation.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not a live market). Suppose an account has $10,000 equity and the trader plans to risk 0.5% of equity, or $50, on one trade.

  • If the stop is 10% below a $1.00 entry, the planned position is $50 ÷ 0.10 = $500 (500 units).
  • If the stop is only 2% below the same entry, the planned position is $50 ÷ 0.02 = $2,500 (2,500 units).

Same dollar risk, different size. If the fill actually occurs 12% below entry instead of 10% because of a gap, the realized loss on the first case is $60, not $50. If a 3.5% round-trip fee applies, a 2% target cannot cover costs even when the price thesis is right.

Risks

  • Sizing does not make a bad market, a scam token, or a leveraged venue safe.
  • Percentage-of-equity rules shrink after losses and grow after wins; that can feel uncomfortable and is easy to override mid-drawdown.
  • USD examples go stale. Recalculate from current equity, current fees and current liquidity.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to trade, to use any particular risk percentage, or to buy any asset.
