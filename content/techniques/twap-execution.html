How it works

TWAP means time-weighted average price. Instead of sending one large order, the method splits the parent order into smaller child orders and releases them over a planned clock — for example, equal slices every few minutes until the size is done. The aim is to reduce the footprint of a single print and to come close to the average price over that window, not to “beat” the market.

Related slicing methods exist (VWAP uses volume rather than clock time; iceberg orders hide size on a book). The common idea is the same: the child size should be small relative to typical volume in that interval, and the schedule should be written down before the first slice.

A careful version usually includes:

  1. A parent size, a window (start and end), and a slice rule (equal notional, or a cap as a percentage of recent volume).
  2. A limit or a marketable limit for each child, plus a rule for unfilled leftovers (re-price, skip, or cancel the rest).
  3. A hard stop if price leaves a pre-set band, so the algorithm does not keep buying a crashing market or selling a ripping one unless that is the intent.
  4. A fee and slippage budget. On a DEX aggregator, each slice is a separate swap with its own price impact. On a CEX, maker/taker fees still add up. See position sizing.
  5. Paper or a tiny live clip first, because venue TWAP tools differ. See paper trading.

TWAP is a better fit for liquid majors and deep pools than for a newly launched token with thin volume. In a thin book, twelve small slices can still walk the price, and each slice can be picked off. For venue differences, see styles by chain.

When it fails

  • The window is too slow for a moving market. Later slices fill at much worse prices than the first, and the average is simply a lagging chase.
  • The window is too fast for the book. Child orders still move the price, which is the problem you were trying to avoid.
  • A predictable clock. If every slice hits at the same second, other participants can lean on the schedule.
  • One slice errors or a connection drop. The parent is only partly filled and risk is not what you planned.
  • Using TWAP as a thesis. Slicing is an execution tool. It does not make a bad trade good.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation). A trader wants to buy $50,000 of a liquid major and chooses 20 equal slices of $2,500, one every three minutes, over about an hour. If mid prices of the fills are $100.00, then gradually $100.80 by the last slice, the TWAP is the notional-weighted average of those fills, not the first print and not the low of the hour. If, halfway through, price drops 8% and there is no abort band, the algorithm keeps buying the decline and the average is pulled down — which may or may not be what was intended. If instead the pair only trades $4,000 in a typical three-minute window, $2,500 children are too large and the “quiet” schedule is still aggressive.

Risks

  • Slicing does not remove market, venue, or slippage risk, and it can increase fee count.
  • Automated TWAP tools can mis-fire around funding, auctions, or halts.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to trade or to use any execution algorithm.
