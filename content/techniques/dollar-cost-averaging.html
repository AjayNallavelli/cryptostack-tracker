How it works

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) is a schedule: you buy a fixed notional of an asset at regular intervals, regardless of the price that day. The aim is to reduce the impact of picking a single entry date, not to guarantee a profit. Published exchange education pages, including Binance Academy’s DCA guide, describe the same idea.

A careful version of the method usually includes:

  1. A written asset, interval and notional (for example a fixed quote amount every week). The size is not supposed to change because the last candle looked “cheap” or “expensive.”
  2. A venue that can actually fill that size, often a centralized exchange for major pairs. Binance (Referral link) is one such venue; it is not the only one, and this is not a reason to open an account.
  3. A plan for failed or stale limit orders (cancel and replace, or use the venue’s recurring-buy tool if you accept its rules).
  4. A record of cost basis, fees and the schedule, so you can see the average price you actually paid. See post-trade journaling.
  5. Paper or a tiny schedule first, so you learn the venue’s recurring-buy behaviour before any larger amount is involved. See paper trading.

DCA is a better fit for liquid majors than for a new memecoin. A “schedule” into a token that can go to zero is just a series of buys into a failing asset. For how venue and fee facts differ by network, see trading styles by chain.

When it fails

  • The asset trends to zero or stays in a long decline. Averaging down does not create a floor. You can finish the schedule with a large unrealized loss, or a total loss.
  • The schedule is abandoned at the worst time. Skipping buys after a drop, or doubling after a rally, is no longer the method being described.
  • Fees and spreads dominate a tiny notional. A $10 recurring buy on a wide spread can be mostly cost.
  • The wrong venue or the wrong token. An illiquid pool, a look-alike ticker, or a wrapped asset you did not intend will not be fixed by “averaging.”
  • There is no end or review. An open-ended schedule can become a larger holding than the account can tolerate.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not a forecast). A trader plans to spend $100 every Monday for 10 weeks, or $1,000 in total, on a liquid major. Suppose the Monday fills (after fees) are $50, $45, $40, $42, $48, $55, $60, $52, $47 and $50. The average fill is the $1,000 spent divided by the units received, not the simple average of those ten prices if each order bought a different amount of the asset. If each order is a fixed $100, you buy more units when the price is lower and fewer when it is higher. If the price then sits at $30, the schedule has a loss; if it sits at $70, it has a gain. The method did not predict either outcome.

Risks

  • DCA does not remove market, custody, exchange, or stablecoin risk.
  • Recurring buys still require you to fund the account and to keep API or app permissions under your control.
