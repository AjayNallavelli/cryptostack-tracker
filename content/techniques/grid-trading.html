How it works

Grid trading places a ladder of buy orders below a mid price and sell orders above it, inside a chosen range. When a buy fills, the method typically places a corresponding sell one grid step higher (and the reverse for sells), so the book keeps working as long as price stays in the range. Exchange education pages, including Binance’s grid-trading explainer, describe bots that do this automatically on a centralized order book.

The method is built for a market that oscillates inside a band. Each completed up-and-down step can realize a small profit equal to the grid spacing minus fees. It is a poor match for a one-way trend: buy orders keep filling as price falls, or sell orders keep filling as price rips, and inventory piles up on the wrong side.

Practical pieces of the method:

  1. Choose a range you can defend with evidence (recent structure or a volatility band), not a guess that “it always comes back.”
  2. Choose the number of grids and the order size so that total inventory, if the range is fully traversed, is a risk you have sized. See position sizing.
  3. Count fees. If the grid step is 0.3% and the round-trip fee is 0.2%, very little is left after a fill pair.
  4. Decide in advance what happens if price leaves the range: cancel, widen, or stop. Leaving a fully long inventory in a crash is a directional bet, not a grid.
  5. Paper the bot first. Venue grid bots have their own rules for funding, leverage and liquidation if the grid is on futures. Binance (Referral link) is one CEX that offers grid tools; this is not a reason to turn one on.

On-chain “grid” ideas exist too, but most published grid playbooks assume a CEX book. For which chains are a better fit for CEX-style majors versus DEX swing trades, see styles by chain.

When it fails

  • A trend, not a range. This is the main failure mode. The grid can finish fully long in a decline or fully short in a rally.
  • Spacing too tight for fees and spreads. The bot is busy and unprofitable.
  • A gap through many levels. Several orders fill at once; inventory and risk jump.
  • Futures grids plus leverage. Inventory can approach liquidation. See avoiding liquidation.
  • Changing the range mid-trade without a rule. That often turns the grid into discretionary averaging.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation). Suppose a trader sets a $90–$110 range on a liquid major, with 10 equally spaced grids (about $2 apart) and $100 at each level, using no leverage. If price chops between $96 and $104 several times, some adjacent pairs may complete and each completed pair realizes about $2 per unit minus fees. If instead price trends from $100 to $70, every buy level can fill, the sells may never fill, and the account holds a large long inventory with an unrealized loss. The bot did what it was designed to do; the range assumption was wrong.

Risks

  • Grid profits in a backtest range are not a forecast. Past ranges break.
  • Exchange-bot risk: outages, parameter mistakes, and the venue’s own liquidation engine on leveraged grids.
