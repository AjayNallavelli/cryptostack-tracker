How it works

Some traders treat the minutes around a token’s first public pool or bonding-curve listing as a separate activity from later “swing” trading. The method is about process and timing, not about picking a winner. Launch venues and slot or block times differ by chain — official fee and speed facts are on the chain pages, and the comparison of which styles fit which chain is in styles by chain.

A careful launch-timing process usually has a written sequence:

  1. Know the venue’s mechanics before the name appears. Bonding curves, migration conditions, and DEX default routes are documented (for example Pump.fun’s curve on Solana; Four.meme-style venues on BNB Chain). If you cannot explain how a buy exits, you do not have a method. See Pump.fun’s bonding curve.
  2. Run a risk checklist first, not after the buy. Authorities, honeypot behaviour, shared funders, and fee floors are covered in the memecoin risk checklist.
  3. Decide size, stop and targets in advance. Launch volatility is large; a stop can be skipped. See sizing and ladders.
  4. Treat attention as a clock, not as a thesis. Public chat, livestreams and scanner spikes can cluster entries. A name that has already had a large, widely seen move is a different trade from a planned process at first listing.
  5. Do not chase an extended print just because you “missed” the open. Missing a launch is a valid outcome.
  6. Journal the process (did you follow the list?) separately from the outcome (did price go up?). See post-trade journaling.

Fast blocks (for example Solana’s 200 ms slots as of the 9 Oct 2026 SIMD-0525 notes, or BNB Chain’s official ~0.45s) change how competitive inclusion is. They do not change the fact that most new tokens fail. Ethereum’s ~12-second slots and documented MEV/sandwich risk make tiny launch snipes a worse mechanical fit than on a low-fee, fast L1 — that is a venue fact, not a reason to snipe somewhere else.

When it fails

  • Bundles and insiders were already filled. The public flow is exit liquidity. See how bundles work.
  • Priority fees and failed transactions. You pay costs and still miss the slot, or you land at a much worse price.
  • Attention dumps. When the livestream or scanner crowd leaves, liquidity can vanish.
  • Migration or router surprises at the curve-to-pool handoff, including tax or tax-like behaviour that was not obvious on the curve.
  • Tooling lag. A scanner print can be later than the people who were already in the pool.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not a real token). A bonding-curve page shows a new name. The pre-written process says: run the checklist (2 minutes), cap risk at a small fixed fraction of equity, refuse the trade if mint authority is live or if a shared-funder cluster is obvious, and refuse it if the name is already many times away from its first print. Suppose the checklist fails on a still-live mint authority. The method stops there. Suppose instead the checklist is clean enough to proceed, the buy fills, and price doubles — then the planned first scale-out is at that 2× level, not a larger improvised target. Suppose price dumps 80% through the stop in one trade: the planned stop was not a guaranteed fill, and the journal records a gap, not “the method failed because I should have held.”

Risks

  • Launch trading is usually closer to a casino than to investing. Most names go to zero. Phishing “official” launch sites are common.
  • MEV, sandwiches, and priority-fee auctions can make the fill much worse than the quoted chart.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to snipe, scalp, or buy any launch. No contract addresses or tickers are listed here on purpose.
