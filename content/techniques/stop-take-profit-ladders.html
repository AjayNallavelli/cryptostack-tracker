How it works

A stop-loss is a resting order (or a venue-side trigger) intended to close or reduce a position if price moves against you by a planned amount. A take-profit is the opposite: a planned exit where the thesis has worked. A ladder splits those exits into steps instead of all-in, all-out. Related ideas also appear on the older exit-lines page.

A typical ladder, described as a method rather than a rule you should follow:

  1. Place the stop when you enter, on the venue, at a level you can explain in writing.
  2. Scale out a first slice at a closer target, then move the stop on the remainder to break-even after fees if the method calls for it.
  3. Take a second slice at a further target, and trail the rest only if a trailing rule was defined in advance.
  4. If one exit fills, cancel or replace the unfilled sibling so you do not flip into an accidental opposite position. Bracket (OCO-style) orders exist on many centralized exchanges for this reason.

Stops come in several flavors: a fixed stop, a bracket that ties the stop and the take-profit together, and a trailing stop that follows price by a set distance. A trailing stop can lock in an open profit in a trend and can also get shaken out in a noisy range.

On perpetual futures, the stop should be thought about next to maintenance margin. A stop that sits beyond the liquidation price will not save the account. See avoiding liquidation. On short-lived tokens, gap risk is large: the next print can be far past the stop.

When it fails

  • Gap and slippage. The order is a request, not a guaranteed price. Thin books and halted or hugely volatile markets fill through the level.
  • The stop never reaches the venue. A note in a chat or a local bot is not a working order if the connection drops.
  • Targets ignore fees. A +8% take-profit after a ~3.5% round-trip cost is a much smaller net result, and can be a net loss after failed attempts.
  • Moving the stop mid-trade without a rule. Widening a losing stop “to give it room” turns a sized risk into an un-sized one. See position sizing.
  • Orphan legs. If the take-profit fills and the stop is left working, you can wake up short (or long) by accident.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not a live token). A trader buys 1,000 units at $1.00 and uses a three-step ladder that some short-horizon methods describe:

  • A stop at $0.65 (35% below entry). If it fills exactly there, the loss is $350 plus fees. If the next print is $0.40, the loss is larger — the 35% figure was a plan, not a floor.
  • Take-profit 1: sell 40% (400 units) at $2.00 (2×). After a 3.5% round-trip cost assumption, the net on that slice is smaller than the headline +100%.
  • After TP1, the stop on the remaining 600 units is moved to break-even after estimated fees, not to the original entry.
  • Take-profit 2: sell 30% of the original size (300 units) at $4.00 (4×).
  • The last 300 units are trailed, for example by a fixed percentage, until stopped.

If price never reaches $2 and the stop fills at $0.65, the trade is a full planned loss. If fees on a small account are a large percentage of the position, this ladder can be a poor fit — the first target has to clear costs on the size you actually trade. The costs guide for small-size Solana memecoin routes walks through that fee floor.

Risks

  • Ladders do not remove rug, insolvency, or liquidation risk.
  • More exits mean more fees and more chances of a partial fill that leaves you with an awkward residual.
  • Past percentage moves (2×, 4×) are not a forecast that any token will make those moves.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to trade or to use these percentages.
