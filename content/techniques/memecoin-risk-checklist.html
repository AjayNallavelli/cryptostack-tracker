How it works

A memecoin risk checklist is a pre-trade filter for short-lived tokens, usually on a launchpad or a new pool. It does not make a token “safe.” It only tries to throw out some of the worst patterns before size is at risk. The items below are methods, not a buy list. Deeper pattern write-ups already live in spotting rugs, how bundles work, and (for Solana bonding curves) Pump.fun’s bonding curve.

Questions a careful checklist usually asks, on the official explorer and on public pool pages:

  1. Mint and authorities. Can someone still mint, freeze, blacklist, or change the metadata? An “abandoned” mint that is not actually abandoned is a common failure.
  2. Honeypot and tax behaviour. Can a generic wallet sell, or only buy? Unusual buy/sell taxes and blacklist hooks show up after the first victims, not always in a banner.
  3. Liquidity. Is liquidity locked or burned, or can a single wallet pull it? Locked liquidity is not a guarantee — locks expire, and other wallets can still dump.
  4. Holder and funder clusters. Many “unique” buyers that share a funder, or a tight holder cluster at launch, are a warning. Visualization tools such as Bubblemaps can show clusters where the chain is covered; they cannot prove intent.
  5. Bundle and sniper patterns. Same-block or same-slot buys from related wallets can leave little float for later buyers. See the bundles guide.
  6. Fees vs the target. If a realistic round-trip cost is around a few percent, a tiny scalp target may not clear costs. The small-size Solana costs guide works an example of that floor (including a Jupiter Ultra standard-route figure as a cost example, not as a venue pitch).
  7. Attention already extended. Chasing a name after a large, already-public move is a different trade from a planned launch process. See timing new token launches.
  8. Size vs the pool. Position size should be a small slice of equity and a small slice of liquidity. See position sizing and ladders.

The same ideas apply on BNB Chain launchpads and on EVM pools, but the buttons and the authority model differ. A Solana mint authority is not the same object as an EVM owner or a proxy admin. Use the explorer for the chain you are looking at.

When it fails

  • The checklist is used as a green light. Passing these tests is not a reason to buy. Most tokens that pass still go to zero.
  • Data is delayed or spoofed. Fake explorers, spoofed socials, and delayed indexers are common.
  • The rug is a pattern the list did not cover — upgradeable contracts, hidden seller wallets after a “renounce,” or off-chain team custody.
  • Speed pressure. Launch chat rooms are designed to skip the list.
  • Size too large for the pool, so even a “clean” chart cannot be exited.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not a real token). A new pool shows 2,000 “unique” buyers in the first minutes and a market-cap screenshot that looks large. A checklist pass might then find that a handful of wallets funded by one source bought in the same slot, that mint authority is still live, and that a 3.5% round-trip cost would already erase a 3% scalp target. The method’s result is “do not size this,” not “short it” and not “buy a different one.” If those red flags are absent, the token can still fail. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Risks

  • Memecoins are often designed to transfer value from late buyers to earlier ones. A checklist is not due diligence of a business, because there is often no business.
  • Tools can be wrong, incomplete, or themselves phishing sites. Bookmark official explorers from the chain pages.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to trade memecoins or any named token. No contract addresses are listed here on purpose.
