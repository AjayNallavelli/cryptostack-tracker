How it works

A post-trade journal is a written record of what you planned, what you did, and what the market did, with enough detail that you can learn from the process rather than from the P&L alone. Published trading-desk playbooks treat the journal as part of the trade’s lifecycle, not as optional notes after a win.

A useful entry usually separates three things:

  1. Plan. The thesis in one sentence, the invalidation, the size, the stop, the targets, and why this trade was allowed under the rules (for example, it cleared a checklist, or it was a scheduled DCA buy).
  2. Execution. Actual fills, fees, funding, slippage, and whether the stop or targets were changed. “I moved the stop” is a process fact, even if the trade later made money.
  3. Outcome in R, after costs. R is the planned dollar risk (see position sizing). A trade that made $80 after fees when $50 was at risk is +1.6R, not “a nice win.” A trade that lost $50 as planned is −1R and may still have been a well-executed plan.

Playbooks often add a fourth line: one repeatable finding. For example “I entered after the move was already extended, which my launch-timing rules forbid,” or “the daily loss brake would have stopped the second trade.” Without that line, the journal is only a diary of emotions. Public desk reviews also suggest judging the process first, then the outcome, so a lucky break of the rules is not recorded as skill.

The same habit supports the desk rulebook and the daily loss brake. It is also how paper trading becomes evidence rather than a story.

When it fails

  • It is written only after wins, or only after losses, so the sample is biased.
  • Fees, funding and slippage are left out, so results look better than the account.
  • The finding is vague (“be more disciplined”) instead of a rule you could apply next time.
  • Private details leak. A public or shared log should not contain seed phrases, API keys, wallet addresses you want kept private, or account numbers.
  • The journal is used to relitigate a rule mid-day instead of waiting for the review.

Worked example

Example (not a recommendation, and not a live trade). Plan: buy a liquid major at $100 with a stop at $97 (3% risk) and a first target at $106, risking $50 (so planned size is $50 ÷ 0.03 ≈ $1,667). Execution: filled $100.20, stop worked at $97.05, fees $4, no funding. Outcome: loss of about $52.50 plus $4 fees ≈ $56.50, or about −1.13R. Finding: “Entry drifted 0.2% from the planned price and I did not shrink size, so realized risk exceeded the $50 cap. Next time, recalc size from the actual fill, or cancel if the fill is outside a 0.1% band.” That finding is usable. “The market was unfair” is not.

Risks

  • A journal will not fix a method that has no edge, and it can create false confidence if you only remember the good notes.
  • Storing trade records on a device or a cloud doc has privacy and security implications.
  • This page is education only. It is not a suggestion to trade.
