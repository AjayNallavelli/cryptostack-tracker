Copying wallets from public "smart money" leaderboards is a popular idea. This page looks at why that tends to make copiers the exit liquidity, and at a small study (Oct 22, 2026) that built a private wallet list the other way round: backwards from coins that already won. For the general concepts, see private smart-wallet lists vs public leaderboards.

Why public leaderboards make you exit liquidity

  • They are watched by copy bots. When a famous wallet buys, bots that follow it buy within seconds. Anyone copying by hand arrives after the wallet and after the bots.
  • The copiers push the price up for the original wallet. That gives the famous wallet a better price to sell into, and the late buyers are the ones it sells to: the exit liquidity.
  • So use them as a crowding warning. If the early buyers on a new coin are mostly famous public wallets, that says the trade is crowded, not that it is good.

The study: a wallet list built backwards from winners

Method

  1. Start from 30 recent pump.fun winners: coins that went from about 22x to about 1,450x from their market cap at roughly 5 minutes old (list below).
  2. Find wallets that bought early in 3 or more of those winners.
  3. Remove wallets linked to a coin's dev or to launch bundles (for example, funded by the same wallet as a deployer or a same-slot buyer).
  4. Apply a full "smart" bar to the rest: 20+ closed trades, net green over the last 25, win rate at least 40%, best coin under 50% of profit, median hold at least 5 minutes.

Result

  • 139 repeat early wallets were found.
  • 3 were removed as dev- or bundle-linked.
  • Only 2 passed the full smart bar. They are called wallet A and wallet B here; their addresses are deliberately not published, because a list that gets published becomes another public leaderboard and loses whatever edge it had.

Caveats

  • The stats cover only a few hours of history (roughly 6 to 9 hours of each wallet's trades on Oct 22, 2026). That is far too short to call either wallet proven; a full week of history is a sensible minimum.
  • One of the two trades seconds apart, so it may be a bot. Bot-like wallets are usually dropped from a list like this.
  • Wallet B only just clears the win-rate bar (40%).

The lesson

  • Public leaderboard wallets: a crowding warning. They are watched by copy bots, which tends to make copiers the exit liquidity. Five of the eleven were net negative over their last 25 trades anyway.
  • A private list built backwards from winners: a confirmation signal, never a trigger. Two such wallets buying a coin early can add confidence to a call that has already passed its other checks. It should never be the reason for a call on its own, especially with only a few hours of history behind it.
