A "desk rulebook" is a short, fixed set of rules that every call must pass before it is named, written down in advance so decisions aren't made in the moment. This page explains, in plain language, the rulebook used for the paper calls on this site (confirmed Oct 22, 2026, and updated that evening), and the reasoning behind each rule. It is an example of how such rules are structured, not a system to copy.

Updated Oct 23, 2026, after the exit study of the first 10 calls: entries are now the first real trade after the call; peaks, lows and real stop fills are logged; rules are judged on average R; extraction is log-only; a 15-minute time stop and three shadow skips are in testing; the trailing stop and pullback-reclaim entry were rejected. Oct 22 (evening): a dev sell alone is no longer a kill; dev graduation history is a score. The status and evidence for every rule are in rules.json and in the table at the bottom of this page.

The four hard gates (all must pass)

  1. Safety. Mint and freeze authority revoked, liquidity safe, and dev and bundle wallet clusters within limits.
    Why: if the creator can still mint more supply or freeze holders' tokens, or a handful of linked wallets hold most of the supply, nothing else on the chart matters. See spotting rugs and how bundles work.
  2. A 5-minute hold after the safety checks pass. Nothing is named for five minutes.
    Why: the first minutes of a launch are dominated by bots and the creator's own wallets. Waiting gives up a little upside in exchange for seeing whether the coin survives once they leave. More in the 5-minute survival hold.
  3. Survival at the end of the hold. All three must be true at minute 5:
    • price is at least 60% of its high so far,
    • unique buyers are ahead of sellers,
    • holder count is higher at minute 5 than at minute 3.
    Why: a coin that has already given back more than 40% of its spike, has more sellers than buyers, or is losing holders is usually being sold into, not discovered. These three checks are a simple way to tell the difference.
  4. Kill it on a bundle or linked-wallet dump, not on a dev sell alone. The coin is out if linked wallets that bought at launch start dumping (a bundle or "consolidation" dump, where one wallet sells far more than it bought because tokens were gathered from other wallets). A dev sell on its own is logged, not a kill, and the survival checks in gate 3 must still pass after the dev has exited. Creator extraction (taking out more SOL than they put in) is now logged only.
    Why: this gate used to kill on any dev or dev-funded sell. In the first 10 paper calls that would have killed a winner (its creator sold early) while missing the dump that actually stopped another. Extraction was added as a kill on Oct 22 and dropped to log-only on Oct 23: the launch checker flagged it on 3 of 3 winners and only 1 of 7 losers, so as a kill it would have removed every winner. What hurts holders is coordinated supply hitting the market at once. More in rug patterns and testing free rug-check tools.

Dev history: a score, not a gate

The creator wallet's past launches are scored: earlier coins that graduated the bonding curve count in its favour, and a long list of launches with none graduating counts against it. The coin being checked never counts towards its own creator's history. Why: one large out-of-sample study (Alphalumina) found that devs with at least one prior graduation completed later launches far more often than devs with none, about 2.1% vs 0.3%. But graduation is not profit, a dev can buy their own graduation, and in the first 10 paper calls dev history did not separate winners from losers. So it adjusts a score and never decides a call by itself.

The target band: about $15k–$40k market cap

Calls are only named in roughly this market-cap range. Why: below it, a coin is usually still in its bot-and-creator phase; above it, much of the early move has already happened. The aim is early coins without rugs; the hold deliberately trades a little upside for survival.

The entry price and what gets logged

  • Entry is the first real trade after the call, logged to the second. Why: for three of the first ten calls (Arnaud, FORM, Pumuckel) the market cap written down at the call was 16 to 20%+ above anything that actually traded, so nobody could have bought at that price.
  • Log the peak, the low before the peak, and the real stop fill for every call. Why: without them you cannot tell whether an exit rule helped, and paper fills at exactly -30% hide how far gaps run.
  • Judge on average R, not win rate. R = net % ÷ 30. Why: a rule that wins more often can still lose more money, and half of the first ten coins ran past 2x at some point, so the size of wins matters as much as how many there are.

The exit line (written before the call)

  • Exit: at -30% from the entry price (the first real trade after the call), on a bundle dump, or as soon as the survival checks fail. A dev sell on its own does not trigger an exit.
  • Take half at 2x, and ride the rest with the same stop.

Why: deciding exits in advance removes in-the-moment hoping. Taking half at 2x roughly returns the original stake (before costs), so the remainder can't turn a winner into a loss. Worked example and caveats in exit lines.

Stops fail on gaps. In the exit study every stop filled below its level, and single sells sent three coins straight through theirs (fills 25% to 61% below the stop). For sizing, assume the real loss when a stop fails is about 50 to 70%, not 30%.

In testing: a 15-minute time stop. Exit at 15 minutes after the call if the price is at or below entry. On the first ten calls it averaged -19.1% against -20.8% for the current exits, a small difference that came from one coin, so it is logged alongside the "no clock exit while buys lead" rule rather than adopted. Rejected: a 40% trailing stop after +50% (worst result, -25.0%, because gaps blew through the trail) and waiting for a pullback-then-reclaim entry (0 of 10 coins showed the pattern).

The PAPER CALL label

Every call carries the label PAPER CALL - not a buy signal, and keeps it until the paper scoreboard shows a positive net result after costs over at least 30 calls. Why: a handful of lucky calls proves nothing. Thirty calls, counted honestly with costs, is a minimum sample before drawing any conclusion. As of Oct 23, 2026 the scoreboard is far from that: 3 wins and 7 losses, -24.0% per call.

The daily brake

After 2 calls in one day go to zero or hit their stop, nothing else is named that day. Why: two dead calls in a day usually means the market, or the reading of it, is off, and that is exactly when the urge to "win it back" is strongest. See the daily loss brake.

The X signal: logged, never a gate

Each call logs yes/no for one X signal: a post that is not from the dev or the project, includes the exact contract, and has 50+ likes or 5k+ views within the window. Follower count alone never counts.

Why: it is worth testing whether real outside attention helps, but not trusting it enough to gate on yet. Follower counts can be bought and say nothing about whether anyone cared about a specific post; in the php case study the creator had about 12.4k followers and the coin still died in minutes.

The size cap

Position size is the smaller of 0.04 SOL or 1% of the pool's liquidity; if that works out under 0.02 SOL, the call is skipped. Why: thin pools can't absorb even a small exit without a big price hit, so tying size to liquidity keeps a position from moving the price on its own, and the hard cap keeps every mistake small. Size as if a gap costs 50 to 70% of the position. At this size fixed priority fees and tips can cost more than the app fee; see trading costs at small size.

Shadow skips (logged, not enforced)

Three filters that looked good on the first ten calls are now logged as WOULD-SKIP on every call instead of blocking it: 30%+ of supply bought by outside wallets in the launch slot, a curve priced in a platform token instead of SOL, and a launchpad or platform-token story. A fourth, RugCheck's largest insider network at 10%+ of supply, is logged as a flag. Why: each one was chosen after seeing the outcomes. Logging them as shadow skips lets the next calls test them without letting an overfitted rule throw away a winner.

Wallet signals and honesty

  • Two or more wallets from a private list buying early is a score booster, never a trigger. Mostly famous public wallets early is a crowding warning. Why in public leaderboards vs a private wallet list.
  • No invented numbers: anything that can't be read is marked unverified, and every call goes in the paper log. If it isn't logged, it didn't happen.
  • After 30 calls, each rule is tested: if coins that passed a rule didn't beat coins that failed it, the rule is a candidate for deletion.
